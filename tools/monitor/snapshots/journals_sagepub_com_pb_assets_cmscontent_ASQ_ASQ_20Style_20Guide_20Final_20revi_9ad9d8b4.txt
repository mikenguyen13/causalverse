Administrative Science Quarterly
Style Guide
I. Introduction
ASQ follows editing and style conventions to help create a consistent presentation among
articles. But style conventions are about much more than consistent practice because they
also reflect an organization’s mission and values. When an organization clarifies the type of
English used in their publications, for example, this ensures that readers and contributors
around the world know what to expect. Such clarification can foster inclusion and respect for
the many different types of English spoken globally. Likewise, making conscious choices to
avoid gender and racial bias in language use can represent another type of effort to be
inclusive.
Even seemingly ordinary choices, like whether to allow first-person pronouns in academic
articles or whether to use the terminal serial comma, can imply values and priorities—
allowing the first person can avoid excessive formality and indicate acceptance of the
author’s subjectivity, while use of the terminal serial comma could signal that clarity is a high
priority. With this in mind, ASQ offers this style guide to help everyone in the ASQ community
understand the links between the journal’s style decisions and its larger mission and values.
The guide also seeks to help anyone interested to learn or refresh their knowledge of some of
the basics of the two key elements of style, diction and syntax, as well as mechanics.
In general, ASQ follows the guidelines of the Chicago Manual of Style, but some of our
conventions have emerged based on our understanding of how best to reach our audience
and on needs specific to our publication format. For instance, we have specific requirements
for figures to ensure readability in printed form. In other cases we deliberately follow different
ideas and organizations that seem best aligned with our values and priorities; for example,
ASQ follows many of the American Psychological Association’s (APA) thoughtful principles
for avoiding language bias related to race and ethnicity.
We detail these and many other style decisions below. If you have questions, concerns, or
ideas about any of these conventions, please feel free to contact Ashleigh Imus (asq-
journal@cornell.edu) to discuss. Because language conventions regularly evolve, we
consider this guide as a work in progress and welcome engagement, suggestions, and
feedback.
II. ASQ Mission and Style
ASQ publishes interdisciplinary scholarship in organization theory. The journal’s articles
reflect numerous disciplines, including organizational behavior and theory, sociology,
psychology and social psychology, strategic management, economics, and industrial
relations. ASQ publishes qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods research. Theoretical
perspectives and topics range from micro to macro, from lab experiments in psychology to
work on nation-states. Moreover, the journal publishes the best theoretical and empirical
articles based on dissertations and on the work of more-established scholars. ASQ thus
reflects great breadth and diversity to speak to a broad audience.
1
ASQ’s mission implies the need for precision, clarity, and economy as principles of language
use in published articles. While these principles are important for any good writing in any
genre, they are critical to scholarly writing intended for a wide, diverse audience.
Style: Diction and Syntax
Diction
Diction is the choice of words, one of the two primary elements of style. ASQ’s
interdisciplinary nature calls for a style reflecting some aspects of the relatively formal register
of traditional academic writing but also the clarity and simplicity needed to reach a broad
readership. The subsections below discuss how authors can use diction to work toward these
qualities and objectives in ASQ publications.
Register
3
Register in writing refers to the variety (e.g., technical) and level (formality) of language used.
Writers use diction to create the correct register for their audience and genre. In academic
articles, the register is usually slightly more formal than in other genres, such as newspapers,
magazines, and blogs. That said, the level of formality even among ASQ articles can vary.
Consider the different levels of formality in the sentences below:
More formal: “Firms increasingly offer employees the opportunity to participate in firm-
sponsored social impact initiatives expected to benefit the firm and employees.” (first line of
abstract from C. Bode, M. Rogan, and J. Singh. 2022. “Up to no good? Gender, social impact
work, and employee promotions.” ASQ, 67).
Here is a less formal version of the same sentence: “More than ever, firms give workers
the chance to take part in firm-sponsored social impact programs expected to help the firm
and employees.” The words in bold are less formal than the corresponding choices in the
sentence above, but both sentences are acceptable examples of academic style. To some
degree, then, a writer’s voice and preferences will determine the level of formality in writing.
Another aspect of register is variety, which can include technical language, or jargon. ASQ
articles may include technical language when appropriate to the discipline. For example, the
following example from a paper on social networks includes a technical term (in bold) specific
to the discipline:
“Cohesive ego networks promote trust and efficiency, allowing focal actors to better mobilize
resources, compared to social structures rich in structural holes.” (from A. Iorio. 2022.
“Brokers in disguise: The joint effect of actual brokerage and socially perceived brokerage on
network advantage.” ASQ 67).
The use of some technical language is acceptable when necessary to communicate field-
specific concepts like the one noted above, but writers should take care to distinguish
necessary jargon from unnecessary new phrases and compressed language that confuse
readers more than they clarify concepts. Creating new technical terms often does not work
2
because such terms tend to separate authors and readers too much. If there is an
established way to communicate a concept, try to use that term rather than inventing a new
one, if possible. Avoiding jargon is especially important for ASQ because it is an
interdisciplinary journal and therefore needs to reflect language that people from many fields
can understand. Obtaining feedback from friendly readers can help authors to identify and
eliminate excessive jargon.
Economy
Avoiding unnecessary words is especially important in academic articles, which often include
complex ideas that may require technical language or special terms. Wordiness can make
conceptually challenging scholarship unnecessarily difficult or hard to process. Think about
how to state your ideas in the simplest terms possible, and avoid these and other common
wordy phrases:
Wordy Succinct
“for the purpose of addressing” “to address”
“a number of” “several,” “numerous”
“a variety of” “various”
“have the capability” “can”
Ordinary and Special Terms
Some of ASQ’s practices regarding common and special terms include the following:
Treat “data” as a plural noun in all cases.
“Dataset” can be written as one word.
Refer to “papers” as “articles,” “studies,” or “research” when they appear in published form.
Use “where” only when referencing a geographic location (e.g., change “We examine studies
where x and y were variables” to “We examine studies in which x and y were variables”).
Rather than “mean reversion,” write “reversion to the mean.”
Rather than “Regression 1,” write “Model 1.”
Pronouns
Like many current publications, ASQ seeks to avoid gender bias in language use. We
therefore respect the use of people’s preferred gendered pronouns, including “she,” “he,” and
“they” for individuals.
3
To avoid gender bias in the use of nonspecific singular pronouns, we also allow and
recommend the use of the nongendered singular “they,” which has long been part of common
English usage.
Example: “When a new scholar joins the organization, they often bring a fresh perspective.”
For those who wish to maintain agreement in noun‒pronoun number, sentences can often be
revised to make the terms plural:
Example: “When new scholars join the organization, they often bring a fresh perspective.”
Race and Ethnicity
Conventions in language representing racial and ethnic groups change as cultures change
and societies reconsider how best to avoid biased usage. ASQ currently follows the American
Psychological Association’s (APA) recommended principles to ensure that publications in the
journal reflect language that is respectful and free of bias toward racial and ethnic groups. We
highlight some of those principles below. Comprehensive details of the APA’s principles can
be found here.
Capitalization and Hyphenation: Capitalize “Black” and “White” when used as racial terms.
Also capitalize terms such as “Native American,” “Hispanic,” “Indigenous,” and “Aboriginal.”
We do not hyphenate multiword names, e.g., “Asian American participants.”
Use of Person
ASQ allows the use of the first person (“I” or “we”) in published articles. We do so to
encourage a sense that authors are participating in a long-term scholarly conversation; they
are responding to past researchers whose work inspired them, and they’re trying to inspire
future research building on their own.
For authors who want to use the first person, be sure to use the first person singular or plural
consistently throughout, depending on whether your article has one or more than one author.
In some cases, you may wish to vary the use of person to fit particular circumstances, such
as when a research assistant or others helped with data collection and coding, but you, the
sole author, interpreted results.
Use of Quotations
Please do not include epigraphs in articles, as ASQ doesn’t print them.
Avoid using long quotations from the literature. A long academic quote takes the reader’s
attention away from the development of your main idea, and scholarly quotes often introduce
jargon. In quantitative papers, if you can improve the readability by paraphrasing the material,
do so.
4
Quotes that are part of your data set are a different story. In qualitative or mixed-methods
papers, quotes often serve an important purpose by letting you show your data rather than
simply telling your reader about it. Select such quotes carefully so you’re using those that
propel your story most effectively while being as concise as possible. You may have quotes
in your data that are interesting but not tightly tied to your arguments; avoid the temptation to
use them, and instead select quotes that help you best show the reader how your ideas and
findings developed.
Introduce all quotations in text so the reader knows who is speaking and under what
circumstances; this allows readers to interpret the quote. If you want the reader to draw
conclusions that aren’t obvious from a quote, make them clear.
Syntax (Sentence Structure)
Syntax refers to the structure and arrangement of words in sentences, including elements
such as word order, subject‒verb agreement, and sentence length. It may be useful to think
of syntax as the traffic rules of language. The categories below can help authors and editors
to understand and follow those rules.
Sentence Length
Sentences in academic writing are sometimes long, in part because scholars are often
communicating complex, nuanced ideas. Yet, the longer the sentence, the more difficult it
can be for readers to process the ideas, and the higher the risk of grammatical ambiguity or
error or of simply introducing too many ideas at once. Try to keep sentences to four lines or
fewer.
Verb Tenses
Standard English has 12 verb tenses, and both speakers and writers tend to shift frequently
between past and present tenses depending on the intended meaning. For this reason, the
use of verb tenses in English can be complex. ASQ tries to simplify this as much as possible.
When writing articles for publication in ASQ, use the past tense to describe methods and
other authors’ contributions (exceptions are acceptable as long as the paper shows internal
consistency). Use the present tense to describe your own findings.
Examples: “For this study, we interviewed 35 CEOs…”
“Sine and David (2010) found…” or “Previous studies have shown…”
“In this study, we find…”
Parenthetical Clauses
Parenthetical clauses can be set off by parentheses, commas, or em dashes (see Mechanics
section below for definition). Scholarly writing has numerous parenthetical elements in the
form of research citations, which are essential to document the claims of an argument. Other
types of parenthetical clauses and statements are usually not essential information and
should be used sparingly because they interrupt the flow of sentences. Too many
parentheticals can make the writing choppy and difficult to process.
5
Restrictive and Nonrestrictive Clauses
Unlike some other forms of English, American English distinguishes between restrictive and
nonrestrictive clauses by introducing the former with “that” and the latter with “which.” A
restrictive clause gives essential information about the noun or phrase to which it refers,
whereas a nonrestrictive clause provides information that is not essential to (i.e., does not
restrict) the meaning of its noun referent. When writing articles for submission to ASQ, try to
observe the correct distinction between “that” and “which” and use each according to your
intended meaning.
Examples
Nonrestrictive clause: “This change, which was largely confined to white-collar occupations,
has leveled off.”
Here, “which” introduces a nonrestrictive clause because the fact that the change was
confined largely to white-collar occupations does not restrict the sentence’s primary meaning,
i.e., that the change has leveled off.
Restrictive clause: “The changes that were largely confined to white-collar occupations
leveled off, but the changes in other occupations increased.”
Here, “that” introduces a restrictive clause because “largely confined to white-collar
occupations” tells readers the types of changes that leveled off; it wasn’t all changes but only
those that were largely confined to white-collar occupations.
Voice
Voice in English refers to the relationship between the action of the verb and the subject and
object of the sentence. When used in this way, voice is a grammatical term, different from the
common usage of “voice” to refer to a writer’s style, i.e., “the writer’s voice.”
In the active voice, the subject of the sentence performs the action; in the passive voice, the
subject of the sentence receives the action of the verb.
Examples
Passive voice: “The data were transcribed and coded in 2020.”
Active voice: “We transcribed and coded the data in 2020.”
Use the active voice whenever possible, and reserve the passive voice for necessary cases,
such as when you don’t know the agent of the verb or when describing a complex process
that may involve multiple agents. The sentence in the active voice above clearly
communicates who transcribed and coded the data, whereas the passive-voice example
leaves this information unclear, which could lead readers to guess or make assumptions.
For more on voice, see Section V, Common Grammatical and Stylistic Errors to Avoid.
Word Order
6
Word order in English is relatively fixed, unlike other languages that are highly inflected. If
English is not your first language, keep in mind that English lacks the flexibility in word order
of some other languages. A good practice is to try to write relatively short sentences (four
lines or fewer) in which readers can easily identify the subject of each verb, including present
participles (verb forms that end in -ing, e.g., “indicating”).
Consider the following construction:
“Building on recent insights into evaluation differences among audiences (Durand and
Paolella, 2013; Goldberg, Hannan, and Kovács, 2016; Zuckerman, 2017), I argue that
audiences’ knowledge and value differences can explain the varying constraints on
producers’ boundary-spanning efforts.”
(from Shi, 2022. “A change of tune: The democratization of market mediation
and crossover production in the U.S. commercial music industry,” ASQ).
The writer has made clear that “I” is the subject of the present participle “building.” But a
common error of this type of construction might look like this:
“Building on recent insights into evaluation differences among audiences, their knowledge
and value differences can explain the varying constraints on producers’ boundary-spanning
efforts, I argue.”
In this case, according to the grammar of the sentence, “building” modifies “their,” but that’s
not what the writer means to say, so the subject of the verb “building” is incorrect. This
creates a common error known as a misplaced modifier (i.e., “building” is misplaced because
it incorrectly modifies “their,” whereas it should modify “I”).
III. Mechanics
Abbreviations
ASQ allows standard abbreviations, which should be written as follows:
i.e., xxxx
e.g., xxxx
cf. xxxx (no comma)
N = number of observations
S.D. (for standard deviation)
d.f. (for degrees of freedom)
Note that “mean” should be written out, not abbreviated as M or capitalized as Mean.
Try to limit acronyms, a type of abbreviation, to those commonly understood, because
excessive use of acronyms can alienate readers and create the effect of excessive jargon.
Spell out acronyms on first use and on subsequent uses if they appear quite a distance from
their initial appearance. When spelling out abbreviations on first use, capitalize only those
terms that should be capitalized as part of their name, e.g., Harvard University Press (HUP),
but chief executive officers (CEOs).
7
Capitalization
Capitalize terms such as “figure,” “table,” “model,” and “hypothesis” when they are specified,
e.g., Model 1; Hypothesis 1, but not when used in nonspecific terms, e.g., “the figure shows,”
or “the hypothesis predicts.”
Do not capitalize words simply for emphasis.
Capitalize initial letters of key words in level-one and level-two headings, e.g., Growing
Normative Pressure.
Capitalize only the initial letter of the first word in level-three headings, e.g., Growing
normative pressure.
Capitalize formal titles when they immediately precede a name, such as Chief Executive
Officer April Smith. Use lowercase for formal titles when used alone, e.g., the chief executive
officers of technology companies.
Capitalize the full names of administrative bodies, e.g., Charles Schwab Board of Directors,
but use lowercase for general names, e.g., the board of directors.
Contractions
ASQ articles generally do not use contractions (e.g., “cannot” rather than “can’t”), which is
consistent with the prose style used in many other academic publications. Contractions are
retained, however, in reported data and quotations. The journal also allows occasional
contractions in book reviews because their style is slightly less formal than that of articles.
Font and Point Size
Use Times New Roman 12-point font for all text. For tables and figures, use at least 8-point
font.
Footnotes
ASQ uses footnotes rather than end notes. Place all footnotes at the end of a sentence after
the period.
Foreign Words
If the word is used frequently in the field, e.g., tertius iungens, a priori, we don’t need to offset with
italics. If the word or phrase is not widely known in the field, we might use a special presentation,
either italics or quotation marks. We decide this on a case-by-case basis.
Hypotheses Statements
Bold the hypothesis number, insert an abbreviated number in parenthesis, and follow it with a
colon:
8
Hypothesis 2 (H2): The tighter the culture…
Italics
Avoid the use of italics for general emphasis.
Italicize the names of variables.
Use italics to introduce new words (quotation marks are also acceptable if authors prefer
this). Use quotation marks to refer to other authors’ terms.
Numbers
Spell out numbers under 11, and use numerals for 11 and above. ASQ allows exceptions
within sections for the sake of consistency, e.g., “We interviewed 25 participants 3 separate
times for 30 minutes in each interview.”
Centuries should be spelled out, e.g., “twenty-first century.”
Use figures for all expressions of percent, e.g., 3 percent.
Use figures for millions and billions, e.g., 3 million
Spell out “percent” in text. The % sign can be used in figures and tables.

Punctuation often seems fairly simple: writers universally understand the need to place a
period at the end of a sentence. But certain types of punctuation, especially commas, can be
complex and reflect wide differences in usage. In editorial terms, the purpose of punctuation
is to clarify syntax, and this purpose guides editors’ thinking about how to use units of
punctuation. The categories below can help authors and editors understand and practice
these conventions.

Colon. A colon often signifies “namely” in a sentence. In general, whatever precedes a colon
should be a complete sentence, and whatever follows the colon should be only the items, list,
sentence, or quotation. To introduce a list or a quotation, use either a complete sentence or a

Correct: “These are the most important data: interviews and field notes based on

Correct: “Participant X observed, ‘we think the small-group meetings help us stay organized.’”
Incorrect: “The most important data are: interviews and field notes based on observations.”
Incorrect: “Participant X observed: ‘we think the small-group meetings help us stay
organized.’”
Use a comma or no punctuation after incomplete sentences introducing hypotheses.
Comma. Comma usage is sometimes a matter of style. For example, ASQ uses a comma
after certain introductory words such as “Thus” or “Therefore.” This is simply the journal’s
style preference.
But editors’ decisions about commas often have to do with ensuring clear syntax. Some of
these syntax rules can be complex and nuanced. The following comma usage rules pertain to
syntax and are thus in a different category:
Use the terminal serial comma (also called the Oxford comma) to separate items in a series
in all cases, e.g., “Our data sources include interviews, observations, and documents from
company meetings.”
Use a comma to separate two independent clauses: “Participants answered most of the
survey questions, and we eliminated from consideration participants who did not answer at
least half of the questions.”
Do not use commas simply to indicate a pause. For example, avoid inserting commas in
compound predicates:
Correct: “We considered the data complete and therefore concluded the collection process.”
Incorrect: “We considered the data complete, and therefore concluded the collection
process.”
That said, do use a comma in a compound predicate when the subject of a clause would
otherwise be unclear.
Correct: “We appreciated that the participants were thorough, and completed the review
process.”
In this case, the comma after “thorough” functions as a separator of syntactic units because it
indicates that “we” rather than “participants” is the subject of “completed.”
Incorrect: “We appreciated that the participants were thorough and completed the review
process.” Without the comma, it’s unclear whether “we” or “participants” is the subject of
“completed.”
10
Commas are also essential for distinguishing restrictive from nonrestrictive clauses (see the
subsection Syntax above in Section II for definitions of restrictive and nonrestrictive clauses).
Nonrestrictive clause: “This change, which was largely confined to white-collar occupations,
has leveled off.” Here, “which” introduces a nonrestrictive clause because the fact that the
change was confined largely to white-collar occupations does not restrict the sentence’s
primary meaning, i.e., that the change has leveled off. The commas preceding “which” and
following “occupations” separate the clause to indicate the nonrestrictive status of the
information.
Restrictive clause: “The changes that were largely confined to white-collar occupations
leveled off, but the changes in other occupations increased.” Here, “that” introduces a
restrictive clause because “largely confined to white-collar occupations” tells readers the
types of changes that leveled off; it wasn’t all changes but only those that were largely
confined to white-collar occupations. The lack of commas keeps the clause tightly connected
to the main subject (“the changes”) and thereby signals the clause’s restrictive nature.
Clauses starting with “i.e.” and “e.g.” should be offset by commas. Don’t use a comma after
“cf.”
Dash. There are two types of dashes: the en dash (‒) and the em dash (—). The en dash is
most often used to indicate numerical ranges, e.g., “The data collection spans 2010‒2014.”
Also use the en dash for compound modifiers in which both terms have equal weight, e.g.,
the “liberalism‒conservatism spectrum.”
The em dash is longer than the en dash and is most often used to highlight parenthetical
statements: “The company has often—but not always—successfully navigated these
changes of management.”
You can use em dashes to set off a series that contains internal commas: “The company laid
out the principles—transparency, purpose, and teamwork—that are essential for these
changes.”
While em dashes are sometimes used in place of commas, take care not to overuse these
dashes. Unlike commas, they often indicate an abrupt shift in thought. Excessive use can
make the writing seem choppy and can result in sentences that are too long.
Ellipsis. An ellipsis indicates the deletion of one or more words in quotations. An ellipsis can
also indicate an incomplete thought.
In Chicago style, an ellipsis is three spaced periods: . . .
Avoid the common error of using an ellipsis to indicate a pause.
Hyphen. A hyphen joins two words, particularly compound terms that precede a noun, e.g.,
“well-documented study.”
11
Use a hyphen to avoid ambiguity, e.g., “The new policy applies to small-business owners.”
Here, the hyphen clarifies that “small” modifies “business” rather than “owners.”
Use a hyphen for other potentially ambiguous words, e.g., to distinguish “re-creation” from
“recreation,” “co-op” from “coop,” and so forth. Don’t use a hyphen for unambiguous words
with double vowels, such as “reexamine.”
Also use a hyphen to clarify phrases such as “much-needed regulation” (as opposed to
“much” in the sense of “abundant” needed regulation) and “more-complex laws” (as opposed
to “more” in the sense of “a greater number of” complex laws).
A hyphen is not necessary for unambiguous compound terms such as “data collection efforts”
and so forth.
As noted above, use a hyphen for certain compound modifiers when they precede a noun,
e.g., “well-documented study,” but when the modifiers follow the noun, no hyphen is
necessary, e.g., “the study is well documented.”
Do not use hyphens for modifiers that are adverbs ending in -ly.
Correct: “These are the most frequently cited laws.”
Incorrect: “These are the most frequently-cited laws.”
Period. In American English, periods (and commas) go inside quotation marks, e.g., “We
completed data collection after six months.”
Insert only one space after periods.
Quotation marks. Per American English, use double quotation marks for quotations: The
company representative reported, “The policy has worked better than we thought.”
Use single quotation marks for quotes within quotes: In the report, the company CEO wrote,
“Our leaders say that the new policy is ‘the best way to gain trust.’”
Use quotation marks only to indicate quoted material and to introduce new terms. Use
quotation marks when the quoted material runs in line with your text. For quotations of four
lines or more, set quotations off from the text and do not use quotation marks.
Don’t use quotation marks to emphasize words or phrases or to indicate distance, irony, or
any other meaning. This tends to confuse readers because the author’s intended meaning is
often unclear. Is the phrase actual quoted material, meant to be scare quotes or convey
some other type of irony, another author’s informal definition, or something else? There is
often no clear way for readers to know. See Section V of this guide for more information.
Semicolon. A semicolon is used to indicate a closer connection between two independent
clauses, compared to the greater separation indicated by a period, e.g., “The company does
not define the policy; managers must define it and inform their teams.”
12
Use a semicolon to separate elements in a list when the elements contain internal commas:
“These principles include lawfulness, fairness, and transparency; integrity and confidentiality;
and accountability.”
A semicolon can also be used to separate long items in a series: “The policy applies to all
regular full-time employees in the designated territories who have been with the company for
at least three years; part-time employees in the designated territories who have been with the
company for at least five years; and all managers in all territories regardless of their tenure
with the company.”
Place colons and semicolons outside quotation marks.
Spelling
ASQ observes spelling conventions for U.S. English as outlined in Merriam Webster’s
dictionary: https://www.merriam-webster.com/.
In quoted material and reported data, maintain original spelling that reflects British or other
types of English, e.g., “labour,” “behaviour,” etc.
Time, Dates, and Time Periods
For time of day, use numerals in all cases, e.g., 1:00 a.m. or 1 a.m.
Write dates as January 20, 2022.
Spell out centuries, e.g., twentieth century, not 20th century.
ASQ uses B.C.E. and C.E. to denote centuries, when necessary.
Type of English
ASQ uses U.S. English for all text but maintains other types of English, if they exist, in
quotations and data. Distinguishing features of this English (compared to British English or
other varieties around the globe) include American spelling and the use of the restrictive
“that” and nonrestrictive “which.” For more information, see the sections above on spelling,
commas, and restrictive/nonrestrictive clauses.
Word Breaks (for proofreading)
Word breaks at the ends of lines: Follow Webster’s for word breaks at the end of lines.
Exceptions: avoid anal-yze.
Headings
Use initial capitalization and bold for key words in level-one and level-two headings. For a

Level-one headings should be in all caps in the page proofs, but in the Word doc, they are

Tables
For table and figure titles, initial capitalize key words in titles (not short prepositions or

Use superscript bullets rather than asterisks. Note that the bullets may be difficult to see

+p < .10; •p < .05; ••p < .01; •••p < .001; two-tailed tests.
If N is the same for all models, indicate N in a note to the table instead of in a table row.
For numbered variables, put each number and variable name in the same column with a
space between them (instead of having a separate column for the numbers).
Figures
Whenever possible, create all figures in black and white, as color does not show up in the
printed version of articles (color does show up online). If possible, use shapes or other

Be sure to make all text in figures large enough (at least 8-point font) so that it is legible to
readers.
IV. Citations and References
This section details ASQ’s conventions for in-text citations and the reference list, which are
different from the most popular styles (Chicago, Associated Press, and American
Psychological Association). We do ask that authors submitting to the journal format both in-
text citations and the reference list according to the following conventions. Additional details
regarding submission guidelines can be found in our Invitation to Contributors.
In-text citations. Try to keep in-text citation lists to no more than three or four references
cited, especially when used to support broad statements. Instead, use “e.g.,” and a
representative sample, or air out the list by noting each reference’s specific context or
connection to the subject.
List multiple references chronologically in parentheses, separated by semicolons.
14
For two or three authors, give all the authors’ last names in text each time the work is cited
(e.g., Haidt, Koller, and Dias, 1993); if there are four or more authors, give only the first author’s
name followed by “et al.” and the date for each citation (e.g., Sasovova et al., 2010).
Page numbers, to indicate a passage in a book or to give the source of a quotation, follow the

V. Common Grammatical and Stylistic Errors to Avoid
The following categories represent a few of the most common challenges that ASQ’s copy
editors see and correct in articles. These issues are not specific to the journal and are
17
common to many genres of writing, so authors may strengthen their writing in general by
focusing on these key areas.
Lack of Agreement Between Subject and Verb
Be sure to check that a sentence’s main verb agrees with its subject. This seems basic, but it
can be easy to overlook, especially in complex or long sentences. Consider this example, in
which the main subject and main verb appear in bold:
The application of the various software programs used for the complex coding processes in
all studies described above was effective.
In this case, the subject and main verb are quite far apart, so it would be easy to mistakenly
write “were” given the numerous plural nouns that appear after the main subject and before
the main verb.
Misplaced Modifiers
This common error occurs when one element in the sentence (usually a present or past
participle) incorrectly modifies (i.e., applies to) a noun. Consider the following example, in
which the modifier and the noun to which it applies appear in bold:
Returning to our point above regarding the time involved in the experiment, participants
took more than an hour simply to prepare for the task, we note.
In this case, the present participle “returning” incorrectly modifies “participants” because the
authors, not the participants, are returning to the authors’ “point above” about time, so
“returning” should modify “we.” Misplaced modifiers such as this one often appear at the
beginning of sentences or at the beginning of new clauses.
Here’s a corrected version of the sentence:
Returning to our point above regarding the time involved in the experiment, we note that
participants took more than an hour simply to prepare for the task.
Dangling Modifiers
Closely related to misplaced modifiers, dangling modifiers occur when an element (usually a
present or past participle) does not clearly modify anything in a sentence. Consider this
example, which is a revision of the previous one:
Returning to our point above regarding the time involved in the experiment, participants
took more than an hour simply to prepare for the task.
In this case, “returning” does not clearly modify anything in the sentence because the
sentence no longer includes “we,” so “returning” is a dangling modifier. We could correct the
sentence by adding “we” to clarify the intended subject of “returning.”
Present Participles with Ambiguous Referent
18
Another version of this problem often appears somewhere in the middle of sentences when
present participles follow a comma. Sometimes in these cases, it can be unclear which noun
the present participle is supposed to modify:
In that study, the authors described how the participants handled time and leadership
hierarchies to complete the task, thereby addressing challenges that had remained
unresolved in the field.
In this sentence, the subject (i.e., agent) of “addressing” could be the authors or it could be
the participants, which means that the present participle “addressing” has an ambiguous
referent. Present participles are common and useful grammatical elements, but they are
prone to this sort of ambiguity, so it’s helpful to check their usage carefully in context. If
there’s potential for ambiguity, restate the intended subject to clarify the meaning. Here’s one
way to correct the sentence above:
Describing in that study how the participants handled time and leadership hierarchies to
complete the task, the authors addressed challenges that had remained unresolved in the
field.
Unnecessary Passive Voice
As noted above in the section on voice, the active voice means that the subject of a sentence
performs the action, while the passive voice means that the subject of the sentence receives
the action of the verb. How can you tell whether a sentence is active or passive? The passive
voice always has a form of the verb “to be” and a past participle. These forms appear in bold
in the example below:
The participants were asked to respond to five questions, and their responses were
subsequently transcribed and coded. The data were then analyzed.
The passive voice is sometimes necessary when the agent of the verb is unclear, when
multiple agents may exist, or when describing a complex process in which the agent is not as
important as the process itself. But writers often use the passive in ways that obscure
meaning because it is unclear who is doing the action. In the sentence above, the use of the
passive voice means that we don’t know who interacted with the participants and transcribed,
coded, and analyzed the data. Was it some of the authors, all of the authors, or a third party
such as research assistants? Whenever possible, use the active voice to clarify these details.
To make the sentence above active, write something like, “We asked the participants to
respond to five questions, and we subsequently transcribed and coded the responses. We
then analyzed the data.
Note: Sometimes people (as well as some automated software programs!) think that
sentences with a form of “to be” and a present participle reflect the passive voice, but they
don’t. For example, the sentence “the data are missing” is not in the passive voice. In this
case, the verb “to be” is a copulative form (also called a linking verb) that connects the
19
present participle “missing” to “data.” In this case, “missing” functions primarily as an
adjective.
Confusing Use of Quotation Marks
Quotation marks intended to convey various meanings have become increasingly common in
scholarly writing, but it’s advisable to reserve quotation marks for actual quotations and to
introduce new terms. When writers use quotation marks for other meanings, readers often
have to guess at the intended meaning. For example, if quotation marks appear around a
word or phrase without documentation (i.e., a page number), the phrase could mean any of
the following things:
• The writer is actually quoting text but has simply forgotten to document the quoted
term with a page number.
• The writer is quoting a data source (which usually does not require page numbers for
documentation).
• The writer is introducing a new term.
• The writer is referring to a term that only a special population knows and uses.
• The writer wants to convey some sort of irony or distancing, as when some people,
such as scholars or another population, use the term but perhaps the writer or some
other unspecified party disagrees or is unfamiliar with the usage.
Note that quotation marks alone do not clearly convey the intended meaning; they simply
force readers to guess, which can create ambiguity and a frustrating reading experience. To
avoid this, use quotation marks only for actual quoted material or to introduce new terms.
Remember to document the quoted material so that readers understand its context, and
when introducing new terms, use phrasing along with quotation marks to ensure that readers
understand that the term is new in the context of the article.
